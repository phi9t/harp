## Opening problem

Given a matrix $A$ and polynomial $p$, how can we understand $p(A)$ without
multiplying large matrices repeatedly? Eigenvectors turn operator evaluation
into scalar evaluation, but nonnormal matrices warn us that eigenvalues alone
do not control norms.

## Conceptual model

The algebra generated by $A$ consists of all polynomial expressions in $A$.
It is commutative even when the surrounding matrix algebra is not. A
diagonalization chooses one basis in which every polynomial in $A$ is
diagonal. Simple spectrum makes interpolation especially transparent, while
density lets later proofs perturb a general matrix to this favorable case and
then take a controlled limit.

## Formal development

### CFT-06-001: diagonal polynomial action {#cft-06-001}

If $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)$ and
$p(z)=\sum_k a_kz^k$, then $D^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i^k)$, so
$p(D)=\operatorname{diag}(p(\lambda_i))$.

### CFT-06-002 and CFT-06-003: diagonalizable calculus {#cft-06-002}

### CFT-06-003 locator {#cft-06-003}

If $A=SDS^{-1}$, multiplicativity gives
$p(A)=Sp(D)S^{-1}$. Each diagonal entry $\lambda_i$ is an eigenvalue and hence
belongs to the spectrum. Notice the geometric factor $S$: a poorly
conditioned eigenbasis can make $\|p(A)\|$ much larger than
$\max_i|p(\lambda_i)|$.

### Minimal polynomials and spectral mapping

The minimal polynomial $m_A$ is the monic polynomial of least degree with
$m_A(A)=0$. Division by $m_A$ shows every polynomial action has a
representative of degree below $\deg m_A$. For polynomials,
$\sigma(p(A))=p(\sigma(A))$; diagonalizable matrices make this immediate, and
the general finite-dimensional proof follows by triangularization.

### CFT-06-004 and CFT-06-005: generated algebra {#cft-06-004}

### CFT-06-005 locator {#cft-06-005}

Every $p(A)$ lies in the algebra generated by $A$, and conversely every member
of that singly generated algebra is $p(A)$ for some polynomial. This exact
representation is special to finite-dimensional algebraic generation.

### CFT-06-006: density of simple spectrum {#cft-06-006}

Over $\mathbb C$, matrices whose characteristic polynomial has distinct roots
are dense. One proof perturbs along a fixed diagonal matrix with distinct
entries. The discriminant of the resulting characteristic polynomial is a
nonzero polynomial in the perturbation parameter, so only finitely many small
parameters are bad.

## Worked examples

For $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=2I+N$ with $N^2=0$,
$p(A)=p(2)I+p'(2)N$. Repeated eigenvalues therefore retain derivative data;
blindly evaluating only $p(2)$ loses the nilpotent contribution.

For distinct $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ and desired values $y_i$, Lagrange
interpolation constructs $p$ with $p(\lambda_i)=y_i$. Thus a simple-spectrum
diagonalization identifies the generated algebra with arbitrary diagonal data
in the same eigenbasis.

## ML bridge

Polynomial filters apply to graph operators, covariance matrices, and
linearized dynamics. Krylov methods exploit the span of
$v,Av,A^2v,\ldots$ without forming $p(A)$ densely. Approximation quality and
nonnormal amplification are separate questions; Crouzeix's theorem will
eventually connect them through the numerical range.
