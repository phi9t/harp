# Chapter 6: Eigenvalues and polynomial algebra

Book: [[knowledge/crouzeix_textbook/crouzeix_textbook_index|Crouzeix foundations textbook]]
Part: [[knowledge/crouzeix_textbook/crouzeix_textbook_index#part-i-linear-structure|Part I -- Linear structure]]
Previous: [[knowledge/crouzeix_textbook/part_01_linear_structure/05_determinants_trace_and_exterior_algebra|Chapter 5]]
Next: [[knowledge/crouzeix_textbook/part_02_geometry_and_calculus/07_inner_product_spaces|Chapter 7]]

## Opening problem

Given a square matrix $A$ and a polynomial $p$, the expression $p(A)$ makes
sense: substitute $A$ for the variable and read $p$'s constant term as a
multiple of the identity. Computing it by repeated multiplication is
expensive, and the whole point of an eigenvector is that it turns that matrix
computation into a scalar one. When does that reduction describe all of $p(A)$,
and what is lost when it does not?

**Motivation.** Two separate questions hide inside "eigenvalues determine
$p(A)$". The first is algebraic: which matrices are the polynomial expressions
in $A$, and is that collection closed and commutative? The second is
quantitative: does knowing $p$ on the spectrum bound $\|p(A)\|$? This chapter
answers the first question completely and shows by explicit example that the
second answer is no. Keeping the two apart is what makes the numerical range
necessary later; a spectral bound alone will not survive a non-diagonalizable
or badly conditioned eigenbasis.

## Conceptual model

Work over $\mathbb C$ with a finite index set $n$ unless a general field is
named. For a polynomial $p=\sum_k a_kX^k$ and a square matrix $A$, set
$p(A)=\sum_k a_kA^k$ with $A^0=I$. The assignment $p\mapsto p(A)$ is an algebra
homomorphism from $\mathbb C[X]$ to the matrix algebra: it respects sums,
products, and scalars. Its image is the *algebra generated by* $A$, written
$\langle A\rangle$. That image is commutative even though the surrounding
matrix algebra is not, because $A^jA^k=A^{j+k}=A^kA^j$.

A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ when $Av=\lambda v$ for some
nonzero $v$; the eigenvectors for a fixed $\lambda$ together with $0$ form the
eigenspace $\ker(A-\lambda I)$. The spectrum is the set of eigenvalues. By the
invertibility criterion of Chapter 5, $\lambda$ lies in the spectrum exactly
when $A-\lambda I$ fails to be invertible, which over a field is exactly when
$\det(\lambda I-A)=0$. That determinant, taken in a formal variable, is the
characteristic polynomial $\chi_A$, so the spectrum is the root set of
$\chi_A$.

Two polynomials attach to $A$. The characteristic polynomial has degree $n$
and is annihilating: $\chi_A(A)=0$. The *minimal* polynomial $m_A$ is the monic
annihilating polynomial of least degree, and it divides $\chi_A$. Division
with remainder by either one shows that every $p(A)$ is already realized by a
polynomial of degree below that divisor's degree, so $\langle A\rangle$ is
finite-dimensional.

A *diagonalization* of $A$ is a factorization $A=SDS^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal.
Not every matrix has one, and the two obstructions are different. The field
may be too small to contain the roots of $\chi_A$, or the roots may be present
but the eigenspaces too small to span. The chapter exhibits one example of
each.

**Historical context.** Naming these objects after their characteristic and
minimal polynomials reflects how they were catalogued once the annihilating
identity was known; we use that vocabulary as a teaching convention. No claim
about who first proved the annihilation identity or in what generality is
being made here.

## Formal development

### CFT-06-001: polynomial action on a diagonal matrix {#cft-06-001}

**Statement.** For a polynomial $p$ over $\mathbb C$ and a family of nodes
$d:n\to\mathbb C$,
$$
p\bigl(\operatorname{diag} d\bigr)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(i\mapsto p(d_i)\bigr).
$$

**Proof.** Diagonal matrices are closed under products and sums entrywise, so
$d\mapsto\operatorname{diag} d$ is an algebra homomorphism from the algebra of
families to the matrix algebra. Substituting $A$ for the variable is also an
algebra homomorphism, and two homomorphisms out of a polynomial algebra agree
as soon as they agree on the variable. Both routes send $X$ to
$\operatorname{diag} d$, so they agree on every $p$; evaluating the family-side
route coordinatewise gives $i\mapsto p(d_i)$. Concretely,
$(\operatorname{diag} d)^k=\operatorname{diag}(i\mapsto d_i^k)$ and the sum
over $k$ is taken entry by entry.

**Boundary and Lean provider.** The index set is finite with decidable
equality; the scalars are the complex numbers because the maintained
formalization fixes that field. Nothing about distinctness of the $d_i$ is
assumed.
[`polynomial_action_diagonal`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L21)
re-exports `CrouzeixConjecture.polynomialEval_diagonal`, whose proof is the
displayed argument in exactly this form: it applies
`Polynomial.aeval_algHom_apply` to the diagonal algebra homomorphism

variable" step, and then evaluates the family coordinatewise. The same

### CFT-06-002: transport through a supplied diagonalization {#cft-06-002}

**Statement.** Let $B$ carry explicit *simple*-diagonalization data: a family
of eigenvalues $\lambda$ that is injective, a change-of-basis unit $S$, and the
identity $B=S\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)S^{-1}$. Then for every polynomial
$p$,
$$
p(B)=S\,\operatorname{diag}\bigl(i\mapsto p(\lambda_i)\bigr)\,S^{-1}.
$$

**Proof.** Conjugation by a fixed unit is an algebra automorphism of the
matrix algebra: it is additive, it preserves the identity, and
$(SXS^{-1})(SYS^{-1})=S(XY)S^{-1}$ because the inner factors cancel.
Substituting a matrix for the variable commutes with any algebra
homomorphism, so $p(SDS^{-1})=Sp(D)S^{-1}$. Applying CFT-06-001 to $p(D)$
replaces the diagonal by its entrywise evaluations.

**Boundary and Lean provider.** The diagonalization is *supplied as data*, not
asserted to exist; the theorem says nothing about matrices for which no such
data is available. Injectivity of $\lambda$ is part of that supplied data and
is what the word *simple* records; the proof above does not use it, so the
identity also holds for a diagonalization with repeated eigenvalues. No
separation hypothesis is imposed on the *resulting* diagonal entries, so
repeated values of $p$ on distinct $\lambda_i$ are allowed.

### CFT-06-003: supplied eigenvalues lie in the spectrum {#cft-06-003}

**Statement.** With the same simple-diagonalization data for $B$ — injective
eigenvalues $\lambda$, change-of-basis unit $S$, and
$B=S\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)S^{-1}$ — every $\lambda_i$ belongs to the
spectrum of $B$.

**Proof.** Write $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)$. The argument has three
steps, and each one is a named library result rather than a fresh computation.

First, similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial. Rewriting $B$
by the supplied conjugation identity and applying characteristic-polynomial
similarity invariance — the result Chapter 1 previews as
[[knowledge/crouzeix_textbook/part_01_linear_structure/01_objects_and_representations#cft-01-005|CFT-01-005]]
and Chapter 5 derives from CFT-05-001 — gives $\chi_B=\chi_D$. The one
bookkeeping step is that the supplied inverse is recognized as the matrix
inverse, so the unit-conjugation form applies.

Second, the characteristic polynomial of a diagonal matrix factors completely:
$\chi_D(t)=\prod_j\,(t-\lambda_j)$. This is the polynomial-ring counterpart
of CFT-05-002.

Third, a product of linear factors vanishes exactly where one factor does, so
$\chi_B(\lambda_i)=0$ for each $i$; and membership in the spectrum is
equivalent to being a root of the characteristic polynomial. Combining the
three gives $\lambda_i\in\sigma(B)$.

**Boundary and Lean provider.** The equivalence between spectrum membership
and characteristic roots needs a field; the maintained statement is over
$\mathbb C$. Injectivity of $\lambda$ again travels with the data and is not
consumed by this proof.
[`diagonalization_eigenvalue_mem_spectrum`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L27)
re-exports
`CrouzeixConjecture.SimpleDiagonalization.eigenvalue_mem_matrixSpectrum`. Its
proof runs the three displayed steps with `Matrix.charpoly_units_conj`,
`Matrix.charpoly_diagonal`, and `Polynomial.isRoot_prod`, closing through
`Matrix.mem_spectrum_iff_isRoot_charpoly`. The general root test over any field
is [`spectrum_iff_charpoly_root`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L43).
The converse inclusion — that the spectrum contains nothing else — uses the
degree count and is not claimed by this card.

### CFT-06-004: polynomial actions lie in the generated algebra {#cft-06-004}

**Statement.** For every polynomial $p$ and every square matrix $A$ over
$\mathbb C$, $p(A)\in\langle A\rangle$.

**Proof.** The generated algebra is by definition the smallest subalgebra
containing $A$. It contains $I$ because a subalgebra contains the scalars, it
contains every power $A^k$ because it is closed under multiplication, and it
contains every scalar combination of those powers because it is a submodule.
A polynomial action is exactly such a finite combination.

**Boundary and Lean provider.** No invertibility, diagonalizability, or
spectral hypothesis is used, and the index set need only be finite with
decidable equality.
[`polynomial_action_mem_generated_algebra`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L30)
re-exports `CrouzeixConjecture.polynomialEval_mem_generatedAlgebra`. That the
resulting collection is commutative is
[`polynomial_actions_commute`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L63).

### CFT-06-005: the generated algebra is exactly the polynomial actions {#cft-06-005}

**Statement.** For square $A$ and $B$ over $\mathbb C$, $B\in\langle A\rangle$
holds if and only if $B=p(A)$ for some polynomial $p$.

**Proof.** One direction is CFT-06-004. For the other, the set of polynomial
actions is itself a subalgebra: it contains the scalars as constant
polynomials, and it is closed under sums and products because $p\mapsto p(A)$
is an algebra homomorphism. It contains $A$, taking $p=X$. Being a subalgebra
containing $A$, it contains the smallest such subalgebra, so every member of
$\langle A\rangle$ is a polynomial action. The representation is not unique:
any two polynomials differing by a multiple of an annihilating polynomial give
the same matrix.

**Boundary and Lean provider.** This exactness is special to a single
generator; for two non-commuting generators the analogous description needs
noncommutative words, not polynomials.
[`generated_algebra_polynomial_representation`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L33)
re-exports `CrouzeixConjecture.generatedAlgebra_mem_iff_exists_polynomial`.
The degree reduction that makes the algebra finite-dimensional is
[`polynomial_action_reduces`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L58),
which rewrites $p(A)$ as $(p\bmod\chi_A)(A)$.

### CFT-06-006: density of the simple spectrum {#cft-06-006}

**Statement.** For every complex matrix $A$ on a nonempty finite index set and
every tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ there is a matrix $B$ whose characteristic
roots are pairwise distinct with $\|B-A\|<\varepsilon$.

**Proof.** Let $\Delta$ be the fixed diagonal matrix whose $i$th entry is the
integer position of $i$ under a chosen enumeration of the index set, so its
diagonal entries are $0,1,\ldots,n-1$ and are pairwise distinct. Follow the
affine segment $A_\eta=A+\eta(\Delta-A)$, which is $A$ at $\eta=0$ and
$\Delta$ at $\eta=1$.

The separation test used is the resultant of the characteristic polynomial and
its derivative. Over $\mathbb C$ a monic polynomial has pairwise distinct roots
exactly when it is separable, separability is coprimality with its derivative,
and coprimality of a monic polynomial with another is exactly invertibility of
their resultant. So define the *bad-parameter polynomial* $q(\eta)$ to be that
resultant computed along the segment; it is a genuine polynomial in $\eta$
because the segment's characteristic polynomial has coefficients polynomial in
$\eta$ and the resultant is a determinant of those coefficients.

$q$ is not the zero polynomial: evaluating at $\eta=1$ gives the resultant for
$\Delta$, whose distinct diagonal entries make $\chi_\Delta$ separable and the
resultant nonzero. A nonzero polynomial in one variable has finitely many
roots, while the real interval $(0,\delta)$ contributes infinitely many
candidate parameters, so some $\eta$ with $0<|\eta|<\delta$ avoids all of
them. At such an $\eta$ the resultant is nonzero, hence $\chi_{A_\eta}$ is
coprime with its derivative, hence separable, hence its roots are pairwise
distinct.

Finally the norm. $A_\eta-A=\eta(\Delta-A)$, so
$\|A_\eta-A\|=|\eta|\,\|\Delta-A\|$. Running the previous paragraph with
$\delta=\varepsilon/d$ for $d=\|\Delta-A\|+1$ — the $+1$ keeps $d$ positive
when $A=\Delta$ — gives $\|A_\eta-A\|<\varepsilon$, and $B=A_\eta$ is the
required matrix.

**Boundary and Lean provider.** Nonemptiness of the index set and
positivity of $\varepsilon$ are both required; the argument also uses that
$\mathbb C$ is algebraically closed, so the characteristic polynomial splits
and the root count matches the dimension. The conclusion is a density
statement about the matrix entries; it does not claim that eigenvectors,
eigenbases, or their condition numbers vary continuously, and in fact they do
not.
[`simple_spectrum_dense`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L36)
re-exports `CrouzeixConjecture.exists_hasDistinctEigenvalues_norm_sub_lt`. The
three displayed stages are three maintained declarations:
`CrouzeixConjecture.simpleSpectrumBadPolynomial_ne_zero` for the nonvanishing
at $\eta=1$, `CrouzeixConjecture.exists_small_simpleSpectrumParameter` for the
choice of a small parameter avoiding the finitely many roots, and
`CrouzeixConjecture.hasDistinctEigenvalues_of_badPolynomial_eval_ne_zero` for
the passage from a nonzero resultant back to distinct characteristic roots.
`HasDistinctEigenvalues` is defined as nodup-ness of the characteristic roots,
which is why separability is the right intermediate notion. Note that the
argument bounds the distance in the induced Euclidean operator norm only; it
says nothing about how far the eigenvectors move.

### Annihilating polynomials and eigenvector transport

Two further facts complete the algebraic picture and are used later in the
book. Every matrix annihilates its own characteristic polynomial, recorded as
[`charpoly_annihilates`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L48);
and the minimal polynomial divides it, recorded as
[`minimal_polynomial_dvd_charpoly`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L53).
The compiled reduction divides by $\chi_A$ rather than by $m_A$, so what it
delivers directly is $\dim\langle A\rangle\le n$. The sharper bound
$\dim\langle A\rangle\le\deg m_A$ follows from the same division argument
applied to $m_A$, using that $m_A$ annihilates $A$; that sharper form is stated
here for the reader and is not separately compiled in this chapter.

The scalar reduction promised in the opening problem is exact on a single
eigenvector and only there. If $Av=cv$ then $A^kv=c^kv$ by induction, which is
[`pow_mulVec_eigenvector`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L69),
and summing over the monomials of $p$ gives $p(A)v=p(c)v$, which is
[`aeval_mulVec_eigenvector`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L78).
When the eigenvectors span, these scalar equations determine $p(A)$ completely;
when they do not, they leave the rest of $p(A)$ undetermined.

Distinct nodes make the scalar data unconstrained: for any prescribed values
there is a polynomial achieving them, which is
[`exists_polynomial_with_prescribed_values`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L130),
supplied by Mathlib's Lagrange interpolation. Combining it with CFT-06-002
identifies $\langle A\rangle$ with all diagonal matrices in the same
eigenbasis when the spectrum is simple. That combination is a narrative
consequence of the two compiled results, not a third compiled statement.

### Two boundary examples

The quarter-turn
$$
J_{\text{rot}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
$$
has no real eigenvector at all. If $J_{\text{rot}}v=cv$ then $-v_2=cv_1$ and
$v_1=cv_2$; substituting gives $(1+c^2)v_2=0$, and $1+c^2>0$ over $\mathbb R$,
so $v_2=0$ and then $v_1=0$. This is
[`planeRotation_has_no_real_eigenvector`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L146).
Over $\mathbb C$ the same matrix is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $\pm i$;
the obstruction was the field, not the operator.

The Jordan block
$$
J=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}=2I+N,\qquad N^2=0,
$$
has its roots in $\mathbb R$ and is still not diagonalizable. The second
coordinate equation $2v_2=cv_2$ forces $c=2$ or $v_2=0$, and in the first case
the first coordinate equation $2v_1+v_2=2v_1$ forces $v_2=0$ anyway. Every
eigenvector therefore lies on the single line $v_2=0$, so no eigenbasis
exists; this is
[`jordanBlock_eigenvectors_lie_on_one_line`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L160).
Consequently the values of $p$ on the spectrum do not determine $p(J)$: the
polynomials $X$ and the constant $2$ agree at the only eigenvalue, yet
$J\neq 2I$. That separation is
[`jordanBlock_action_needs_more_than_values`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L172).
The correct formula keeps the derivative data: $p(J)=p(2)I+p'(2)N$.

## Worked examples

For $J=2I+N$ above and $p(z)=z^3$, expanding with $N^2=0$ gives
$$
J^{3}=(2I+N)^3=8I+3\cdot4N=\begin{bmatrix}8&12\\0&8\end{bmatrix},
$$
in agreement with $p(2)=8$ and $p'(2)=12$. Evaluating only at the eigenvalue
would have produced $8I$ and lost the entire off-diagonal entry.

The cumulative family is the same object with the eigenvalue and the shear
left free:
$$
A_{\lambda,\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&\alpha\\0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}
=\lambda I+\alpha N .
$$
Its characteristic polynomial is $(t-\lambda)^2$ for every $\alpha$, so the
whole family shares one spectrum, in agreement with the two invariants
computed in Chapter 5. For $\alpha\neq0$ the minimal polynomial is
$(t-\lambda)^2$ as well and the matrix is not diagonalizable; for $\alpha=0$
the minimal polynomial drops to $t-\lambda$. A single family therefore crosses
the diagonalizability boundary while its spectrum never moves, which is why
the coming chapters measure $A_{\lambda,\alpha}$ with norms instead.

For distinct nodes the situation is the opposite. Taking
$D=\operatorname{diag}(3,5)$ and prescribing values $y_1,y_2$, Lagrange
interpolation produces $p$ with $p(3)=y_1$ and $p(5)=y_2$, so $\langle
D\rangle$ is the full two-dimensional algebra of diagonal matrices.

## ML bridge

**Mathematical object.** A polynomial filter on a graph or covariance
operator is exactly a member of the algebra generated by that operator, and
CFT-06-005 says the two descriptions coincide. A Krylov subspace
$\operatorname{span}(v,Av,\dots,A^{k}v)$ is the image of that algebra's
low-degree part applied to a single vector.

**Exact transfer.** CFT-06-002 is what lets a filter be applied in the
eigenbasis: diagonalize once, evaluate the scalar filter on each eigenvalue,
and transport back. The degree reduction of CFT-06-005 is what bounds how many
distinct filters a fixed operator can realize: at most $\deg m_A$ independent
ones, so increasing polynomial degree past that point adds no new operators.

**Non-transfer.** None of this bounds $\|p(A)\|$ by
$\max_i|p(\lambda_i)|$. The transport identity carries the conditioning of the
eigenbasis with it, and the Jordan example above has no eigenbasis at all. A
filter designed to be small on the spectrum can therefore act with large norm,
which is the practical face of nonnormality. Nothing here addresses
floating-point eigendecomposition error or the stability of Krylov recurrences
either.

**Calculation.** Let $p(z)=z-2$, which vanishes on the spectrum of $J$. Then
$\max_i|p(\lambda_i)|=0$ while $p(J)=N$ has operator norm $1$. The gap between
the spectral maximum and the true norm is unbounded in general: for
$A_{2,\alpha}$ the same polynomial gives $p(A_{2,\alpha})=\alpha N$, whose norm
is $|\alpha|$. Crouzeix's theorem will restore a bound of this shape by
replacing the spectrum with the numerical range.

## Lean translation

`Polynomial.aeval A p` denotes $p(A)$; the maintained Crouzeix formalization
wraps it as `CrouzeixConjecture.polynomialEval p A` over
`SquareMatrix n = Matrix n n ℂ`. `Matrix.charpoly` is $\chi_A$ and `minpoly K A`
is $m_A$; `spectrum K A` is the spectrum and `Matrix.diagonal` builds
$\operatorname{diag} d$. Eigenvector equations are written with `Matrix.mulVec`
as `A *ᵥ v = c • v`.

The six public cards of this chapter are exact re-exports of maintained
`CrouzeixConjecture` theorems rather than new proofs, and their contract rows
name the underlying declaration explicitly. `SimpleDiagonalization` is
diagonalization data supplied as a structure; `HasDistinctEigenvalues B` is the
predicate that $\chi_B$ has no repeated root. The support declarations listed
above are proved in this chapter over an arbitrary field where the argument
allows it, which is why they take `[Field K]` rather than fixing $\mathbb C$.

The six exercise theorems live in
`CrouzeixTextbook.Part01.Exercises.Chapter06` in the
[chapter Lean source](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean).
None of them is an alias to a public card: two are concrete real
computations, two are general field-level statements about eigenvectors and
conjugation, one is the repeated-eigenvalue separation, and one instantiates
the density theorem at a fixed index type.

## Exercises

### CFT-06-E01 -- retrieval {#exercise-cft-06-e01}

State what it means for $\lambda$ to be an eigenvalue with eigenvector $v$,
and verify the definition on $D=\operatorname{diag}(3,5)$: exhibit
eigenvectors for both eigenvalues and show that every eigenvector for the
eigenvalue $3$ has vanishing second coordinate.

**Solution.** The condition is $Dv=\lambda v$ with $v\neq0$; the eigenspace is
$\ker(D-\lambda I)$ and the spectrum is the set of such $\lambda$. The
standard basis vectors work: $De_1=3e_1$ and $De_2=5e_2$. If $Dv=3v$ then the
second coordinate satisfies $5v_2=3v_2$, hence $v_2=0$.
[`exercise_01_solution`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L186)
checks the two eigenvector equations and the universal statement about the
eigenspace.

### CFT-06-E02 -- calculation {#exercise-cft-06-e02}

Compute $p(J)$ for the Jordan block $J$ above and $p(z)=z^3$, both as a
polynomial action and as a matrix power, and check the two agree.

**Solution.** Writing $J=2I+N$ with $N^2=0$ and expanding gives
$8I+12N$, so
$$
J^{3}=\begin{bmatrix}8&12\\0&8\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The polynomial action reduces to that power because substituting a matrix for
the variable is a ring homomorphism, so it carries $X^3$ to the cube.
[`exercise_02_solution`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L200)
states both forms as two conjuncts and derives the first from the second
exactly as described, through `map_pow` and `Polynomial.aeval_X`. The
off-diagonal entry $12=p'(2)$ is the derivative data that scalar evaluation
alone discards.

### CFT-06-E03 -- written-proof {#exercise-cft-06-e03}

Over an arbitrary field, prove that a polynomial action preserves an
eigenvector: if $Av=cv$ then $p(A)v=p(c)v$ for every polynomial $p$. State the
power case separately, and deduce that an eigenvector is annihilated by $p(A)$
whenever $p$ vanishes at its eigenvalue.

**Solution.** Induct on the exponent for $A^kv=c^kv$, using
$A^{k+1}v=A^k(Av)=A^k(cv)=c\,A^kv$. Then induct over the monomial
decomposition of $p$: sums are handled by additivity of $v\mapsto Mv$ in $M$,
and the monomial $aX^k$ contributes $a\,A^kv=ac^kv$. For the last conclusion,
substitute $p(c)=0$ into $p(A)v=p(c)v$ and use $0\cdot v=0$.
[`exercise_03_solution`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L210)
records all three conclusions for any field, any finite index type, any matrix,
any vector, and any scalar. The third is the fact the density argument of
CFT-06-006 ultimately needs: a polynomial that kills every eigenvalue kills
every eigenvector.

### CFT-06-E04 -- written-proof {#exercise-cft-06-e04}

Derive the diagonalizable polynomial formula from a one-sided inverse: assume
only $RS=I$ and prove that
$p\bigl(S\operatorname{diag}(d)R\bigr)=S\operatorname{diag}\bigl(i\mapsto p(d_i)\bigr)R$
over an arbitrary field.

**Solution.** First transport powers. A one-sided matrix inverse is two-sided,
so $(SXR)^k=SX^kR$ by induction, the inner $RS$ collapsing at each step. Sums
and scalars pass through because $X\mapsto SXR$ is linear, so
$p(SXR)=Sp(X)R$ for every polynomial. Applying the diagonal evaluation of
CFT-06-001 to $p(\operatorname{diag} d)$ finishes the calculation.
[`exercise_04_solution`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L220)
states the field-level identity. Compared with CFT-06-002 it drops the unit

### CFT-06-E05 -- boundary {#exercise-cft-06-e05}

Give a repeated-eigenvalue example whose polynomial action is not determined
by the values of the polynomial on the spectrum, identify the residual data
explicitly, and show that its eigenvectors are confined to one line.

**Solution.** Take the Jordan block $J$. Its only eigenvalue is $2$, and the
polynomials $X$ and the constant $2$ agree there; but $J\neq2I$, since the
entry in position $(1,2)$ is $1$ on the left and $0$ on the right. The residual
data is the difference: $(X-2)(J)=J-2I=N$, the nilpotent part. Every
eigenvector has vanishing second coordinate, so the eigenvectors span a line
rather than the plane and no eigenbasis exists.
[`exercise_05_solution`](../../../formalization/lean/CrouzeixTextbook/Part01/Chapter06.lean#L227)
states the agreement on the spectrum, the inequality of the two actions, the
explicit identification of $(X-2)(J)$ with the nilpotent matrix unit, and the
confinement of the eigenvectors as four conjuncts. The third is checked
entrywise, so no step of it is left to the reader.

### CFT-06-E06 -- lean-proof {#exercise-cft-06-e06}

Instantiate the density theorem at the fixed index type `Fin 2` and identify
the hypotheses that must be discharged to do so.

**Solution.** The theorem needs a finite index type with decidable equality,
a nonempty index, and a strictly positive tolerance. All three are available
for `Fin 2` by instance search and by the assumed $\varepsilon>0$, so the
specialization is immediate.

## Synthesis and forward dependencies

Part I is now complete. Operators have been separated from coordinates,
kernels and ranges give the exactness bookkeeping, duality explains
transposes, Chapter 5 supplies the two similarity invariants and the
invertibility criterion, and this chapter turns polynomials into operators and
identifies the algebra a single matrix generates.

The chapter also fixed the limitation that the rest of the book is organized
around: the spectrum is stable and easy, and it does not control size. Part II
adds the geometry needed to measure that gap — inner products, adjoints,
positivity, singular values — and the density result proved here returns in
the final limiting arguments, where a general matrix is perturbed to the
simple-spectrum case and the bound is passed to the limit.
