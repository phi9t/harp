# Reading Science Acceleration as Case-Study Evidence

## Problem

Captured lines 23–34 present a company-authored report of short scientific collaborations, not a controlled benchmark. It asks where GPT-5 saved expert time, produced useful research steps, failed, and still required human input.

## Core mechanism

Lines 35–60 and 94–102 organize 13 titled case-study sections into four chapters: frontier rediscovery, deep literature search, tandem work, and new results. Experts supply problems, data, prompts, scaffolds, corrections, and verification; documented variants include GPT-5, GPT-5 Pro, and scaffolded GPT-5.

## Reported evidence

From the abstract and 13-section main report, not a benchmark aggregate, the authors report cases across mathematics, physics, astronomy, computer science, biology, and materials science. The abstract says four new mathematics results were carefully verified by human authors. Other support is author-described interactions, transcripts, and case-study outcomes rather than a shared metric or controlled comparison. Harp did not reproduce these results.

## Key limitation

Lines 81–90 warn that GPT-5 confidently makes and defends mistakes, is sensitive to initial and follow-up prompts, and can be hard to reproduce. Chapter IV also records rejected incorrect proofs and human-supplied proof details at lines 3236–3245 and 3870–3879.

## Why Weng cites it

Weng lists this report only as reference 29 at lines 457–458 and gives no prose citation rationale. Harp’s source-derived interpretation is that it bounds science-acceleration discussion with expert-mediated case evidence, not autonomous science or recursive self-improvement.
